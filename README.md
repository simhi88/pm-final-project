## Step 3 · Craft your hypothesis
- **Qualitative evidence (from M2) , quote the specific friction / moment of misery for your persona:** The brief's own description of Mara: "She isn't sure whether a plan would be affordable, why she's being asked about her income, what happens if she misses a payment... If the journey confuses her or asks for documents she can't easily find, she calls an agent." Research themes 1 to 3 back this up: consumers don't know what is affordable, don't understand why information is requested, and fear the consequences of a wrong answer or a missed payment.
- **Quantitative evidence (from M3) , name the metric or data point that confirms the pain; cite the number:** 31% of journey starters contact an agent within 7 days. 28% of uploaded income documents need clarification or review. 19% of new agreements miss a payment within 60 days. Only 46% of started journeys end in an agreement.
- **Persona , role, goal, and the friction you confirmed in the reconciliation steps:** Role: Mara, 38, a shift worker with variable income and two open claims, who opens the portal on her phone after a digital reminder.

Goal: To agree on an instalment plan she can keep, covering both claims, without making her situation worse.

Friction: At the income step she can't find documents that fit shift-based income and doesn't know why they're needed or what her answer commits her to. Fearing a wrong answer could backfire, she stops and calls an agent.
- **Problem you are solving , one sentence describing the specific friction this initiative removes:** Consumers like Mara abandon the instalment journey and call an agent because the income step doesn't explain why information is needed, what is affordable, or what a missed payment means.
- **Strategic outcome , what behaviour change do you expect, and how does it map to retention / revenue / churn?:** Fewer variable-income consumers abandon and call, and more complete a plan they understand. For Riverty this maps to less avoidable agent work, more agreements that hold, and better lifetime value on purchased portfolios. In this case the equivalent of retention, revenue and churn is plan completion, plan survival and recovery performance. For client-owned receivables, it also means more responsible, transparent servicing.
- **Primary success metric (initiative signal) , the leading indicator that tells you the gap is closing:** The share of journey starters who contact an agent within 7 days, baseline 31%, measured in the pilot segment against a comparison group.
- **Guardrail metric (product signal) , the metric that must NOT drop; it protects your existing base:** The 60-day missed-payment rate on new agreements must not rise above the 19% baseline. This protects against SSR improving while plans quietly break.
- **Decision window , how much time or data before you scale, pivot, or kill? minimum threshold to proceed?:** - Minimum window: about 10 weeks per consumer cohort, because the guardrail needs 60 days of post-agreement data. Instrumentation must exist before launch to establish a clean baseline.
- Scale: the 7-day contact rate falls meaningfully (proposed: at least 5 points) and the 60-day missed-payment rate is at or below 19% in every tracked subgroup.
- Pivot: contact falls but missed payments rise, or contact doesn't move, which means the problem sits elsewhere, such as documents or offer logic.
- Kill or pause: the guardrail worsens or a fairness issue appears in any subgroup.
- **Draft your full hypothesis sentence , one to three sentences; quote the metric, name the persona, name the outcome:** Based on the brief's description of Mara stalling at the income step and calling an agent, and on 31% of journey starters contacting an agent within 7 days, I believe that a guided portal journey explaining why income is requested, what is affordable and what a missed payment means, for variable-income consumers with multiple claims like Mara, will result in more of them agreeing a plan without calling an agent, as measured by a 5-point reduction (31% to 26%) in 7-day agent contact among journey starters. I will protect the 60-day missed-payment rate on new agreements (19% baseline, must not rise in any tracked subgroup). I will make a go/no-go decision after 10 weeks of pilot data in Germany, which allows 60 days of post-agreement follow-up.

### Slide 10 · Story
- **Friction + aha:** - She doesn’t know why the information is needed
- She fears a wrong answer will backfire
- She can’t find the documents
- Her shift-based income fits no single figure
- She doesn’t know if a plan is affordable or what a missed payment means
- **Takeaways / next:** Key takeaways:
- “Why we ask” fixes half of the moment of misery. It answers “why is this needed?” It does not answer “where do I find a document?” or “my income fits no payslip.” Document guidance and variable-income input are what the 31% to 26% target also depends on.
- Variable-income input is the biggest gap in the roadmap. I scored it Value 5, and it isn’t scheduled. Without it, Q2 still asks Mara for one typical figure.
- The critical path is approval, not engineering. The build is copy plus components. Legal and Ops sign-off on every sentence sets the release date.
- The “how it’s used” line carries the most value and the most risk. It addresses her fear that a wrong answer will backfire. But “a close estimate is fine” can invite rough figures and put the 19% guardrail at risk, so it ships only if Ops confirms it’s true today.
- Neutral wording matters. Any hint that a higher or lower figure helps her changes behaviour, so the copy can’t suggest it.
- You can’t prove the effect yet. Income-step drop-off has no baseline, so the instrumentation dashboard has to ship first, with a holdout to separate this feature from the other Now items.
- Five test participants give a direction, not proof. The comprehension and time-on-task evals tell you whether to keep going, not whether you’ve hit the target.

"Aha" moment:
The aha moment is the moment Mara realises the income question is not a test. It happens at Q2, the typical-month question, because her variable income makes it the hardest to answer. She reads “We ask so we can suggest payments that fit a normal month for you. A close estimate is fine.” Within seconds she stops treating the figure as a trap, types an approximate number, and continues without calling an agent.
